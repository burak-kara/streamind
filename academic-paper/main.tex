\begin{abstract}
    Real-time meeting intelligence requires streaming summarization systems that produce coherent outputs under strict latency constraints.
    This paper presents our approach to the STREAMIND Grand Challenge: a seven-node Juturna pipeline that ingests live Opus/\gls{rtp} audio via the Janus \gls{webrtc} gateway, transcribes incrementally with faster-whisper, deduplicates overlapping \gls{asr} chunks, and summarizes \SI{300}{\second} rolling windows with Qwen3.5-4B-AWQ~\cite{qwen3-5-4b-awq} via vLLM.
    Three contributions address the challenge's joint optimization of summary quality, keyword relevance, and processing latency: (1)~streaming chunk deduplication preserves temporal coherence across overlapping \gls{asr} outputs; (2)~a keyword guarantee algorithm floors the worst-case keyword penalty at zero; and (3)~quality-gated latency optimization through prompt engineering and thinking-mode suppression achieves average processing times of \SI{0.79}{\second} while maintaining summary quality at ceiling.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
    real-time speech processing, automatic speech recognition, large language models, streaming summarization, WebRTC, Juturna
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\glsresetall

Live-audio intelligence systems that produce real-time summaries of meetings, podcasts, and conference sessions are increasingly valuable as remote communication grows.
Unlike batch summarization, which operates on complete transcripts after the fact, streaming summarization must produce incremental outputs under strict latency constraints while maintaining coherence across overlapping audio segments.
The STREAMIND Grand Challenge~\cite{streamind-challenge} formalizes this task: systems receive a live Opus-encoded mono audio stream via \gls{rtp}, produce structured summaries and keywords every \SI{300}{\second}, and are scored on summary quality, keyword relevance, and processing latency.

The challenge defines a per-chunk composite score $C_i = B_i + K_i + L_i$ that creates a non-trivial optimization landscape.
$B_i$ sums five Likert-scale quality criteria (factual consistency, relevance, coherence, fluency, and conciseness), ranging from \num{5} to~\num{25}.
$K_i = 2 \cdot (N_{\text{rel}} - N_{\text{irr}})$ evaluates each of exactly three keywords, ranging from $-6$ to~$+6$.
The latency bonus $L_i = 10 \cdot e^{-0.5 \cdot t_{\text{proc}}}$ rewards fast processing but is \emph{gated} on $B_i \ge 10$: below this threshold, $L_i = 0$ regardless of speed (\cref{fig:scoring}).
This creates a hard cliff\td a pipeline optimized for raw speed that produces $B_i = 9$ scores strictly worse than a slower pipeline achieving $B_i = 15$.
A flat bonus of $+4$ is awarded for using the Janus \gls{webrtc} gateway~\cite{janus} as the audio source.
These constraints dictate our design principle: \emph{quality first, latency second, keyword discipline always}.

We make three contributions, each targeting a specific scoring term:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \item A streaming chunk deduplication strategy that preserves temporal coherence across overlapping \SI{5}{\second} \gls{asr} chunks while keeping the aggregated \SI{300}{\second} window non-redundant.
    \item A keyword guarantee algorithm that strips banned generic terms and back-fills missing slots from transcript proper nouns, converting the worst-case $K_i = -6$ into a floor of $K_i = 0$.
    \item Quality-gated latency optimization through prompt engineering and thinking-mode suppression, achieving average processing times of \SI{0.79}{\second} ($L_i \approx 6.8$) while maintaining $B_i$ at \num{24}--\num{25} of \num{25}.
\end{enumerate}
\Cref{sec:related} surveys related work and background.
\Cref{sec:architecture} details the pipeline architecture.
\Cref{sec:implementation} covers deployment.
\Cref{sec:evaluation} presents evaluation results.
\Cref{sec:discussion} discusses limitations and future directions.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Juturna~\cite{juturna} is an open-source Python library for fast prototyping of real-time multimedia \gls{ai} pipelines.
Pipelines are defined declaratively as directed graphs of nodes via JSON configuration; Juturna compiles this configuration into a running dataflow at startup and manages each node's lifecycle through the stages \texttt{configure()} $\to$ \texttt{warmup()} $\to$ \texttt{start()} $\to$ \texttt{stop()}.
Nodes exchange typed messages along directed edges; the library distinguishes three conceptual roles: \emph{source} nodes originate data from external streams, files, or \glspl{api}; \emph{processing} (proc) nodes transform incoming messages and emit results downstream; and \emph{sink} nodes deliver terminal data to external destinations.
Each node runs up to three independent threads\td for inbound queuing, data processing, and (for source nodes) data generation\td enabling stages to be developed and replaced without disrupting adjacent nodes.
Janus~\cite{janus} is an open-source, general-purpose \gls{webrtc} server that accepts client sessions, performs \gls{webrtc} negotiation, and forwards the decoded media as plain \gls{rtp} to downstream consumers.
Juturna ships a built-in \texttt{audio\_rtp} source node for this exact integration pattern; in our pipeline it subscribes to Janus's \gls{rtp} endpoint, receiving mono Opus/\SI{48}{\kilo\hertz} audio~\cite{rtp,opus} with identical code paths in development and evaluation and earning the flat $+4$ bonus.

For \gls{asr}, the Whisper model family~\cite{whisper} has demonstrated strong transcription performance across languages and acoustic conditions.
We use faster-whisper~\cite{faster-whisper}, which reimplements Whisper inference via CTranslate2 with int8 quantization for reduced memory footprint and latency.
In streaming mode, audio is chunked into short overlapping segments and transcribed independently with previous-text conditioning disabled to prevent context drift across chunks.

Small instruction-tuned \glspl{llm} can produce structured JSON outputs (summaries and keywords) in a single inference call~\cite{qwen3}.
vLLM~\cite{vllm} provides efficient \gls{gpu} inference through paged attention and continuous batching, enabling in-process model loading without daemon overhead.
For evaluation, \gls{llm}-as-judge methods~\cite{geval} score generated summaries against source transcripts on multiple quality dimensions; using a panel of judges from distinct model families mitigates the single-model bias inherent to any one judge.
We position STREAMIND as a \emph{systems} contribution that composes these building blocks into a scoring-formula-aware pipeline, rather than proposing new models or training procedures.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\Cref{fig:pipeline} shows the end-to-end pipeline architecture.
Seven Juturna nodes process audio from \gls{rtp} ingestion through structured output transmission.
Every component is justified against one or more terms of the scoring formula $C_i = B_i + K_i + L_i$: audio reception secures the $+4$ Janus bonus, hallucination filtering and novel extraction defend~$B_i$, the summarizer jointly optimizes~$B_i$ and~$L_i$, and keyword validation guards~$K_i$.

\subsection{Audio Reception}
\label{sec:arch:audio}

Janus~\cite{janus} accepts the challenge \gls{webrtc} session and forwards mono Opus/\SI{48}{\kilo\hertz} \gls{rtp} media to Juturna's built-in \texttt{audio\_rtp} source node on \gls{udp} port~\num{8888}.
The pipeline declares \texttt{payload\_type:~97} and \texttt{encoding\_clock\_chan: "opus/48000/1"} matching the challenge specification.
Using Janus in both development and evaluation ensures identical code paths and earns the flat $+4$ bonus at zero architectural cost.

\subsection{Incremental ASR}
\label{sec:arch:asr}

The \texttt{audio\_chunker} node buffers the continuous audio stream into \SI{5}{\second} chunks with \SI{1}{\second} overlap, yielding a \SI{4}{\second} advance per step (\cref{fig:chunk-overlap}).
Each chunk is transcribed by the \texttt{transcriber\_whisper} node running faster-whisper~\cite{faster-whisper} with int8 quantization on \gls{cpu}, reserving \gls{gpu} \gls{vram} entirely for the summarizer.
Previous-text conditioning is disabled to prevent hallucination propagation across chunks, and \gls{vad} filtering via Silero~\cite{silero-vad} skips silence segments.

\begin{figure}[htb!]
    \centering
    \input{figures/chunk-overlap}
    \caption{Audio chunking and novel extraction. Five-second chunks slide by \SI{4}{\second}; the novel extractor isolates newly-spoken content each step, discarding the \SI{1}{\second} overlap with the previous chunk.}
    \label{fig:chunk-overlap}
\end{figure}

We evaluated two model variants on \num{19} windows of a \num{36}-minute podcast episode (\cref{tab:asr-comparison}): \texttt{small.en} (\SI{244}{\M} parameters, English-only) and \texttt{large-v3-turbo} (\SI{809}{\M}, multilingual distillation of large-v3).
Both achieve identical average \gls{wer} of \num{0.69}, yet \texttt{small.en} produces a higher composite score ($\bar{C} = 36.00$ vs.\ $35.73$) due to superior keyword stability ($\bar{K} = 4.9$ vs.\ $4.5$).
This demonstrates that transcription accuracy is not the pipeline bottleneck: downstream keyword extraction stability has a larger impact on the final score than raw \gls{wer}.

\subsection{Hallucination Filter}
\label{sec:arch:halluc}

The \texttt{hallucination\_filter} node applies two post-\gls{asr} guards.
First, inline noise tags (\texttt{[Music]}, \texttt{(applause)}) are stripped via regex patterns.
Second, the cleaned text is checked against \num{11} known hallucination patterns (\eg \quotes{thank you for watching}, \quotes{please subscribe}) and silently dropped if matched.
Without this filter, hallucinated sentences reach the summarizer and are faithfully summarized as facts, directly harming~$B_i$ factual consistency.

\subsection{Novel Chunk Extraction}
\label{sec:arch:novel}

The \SI{1}{\second} overlap between consecutive chunks means that roughly one second of speech appears in both the previous and current transcription.
The \texttt{novel\_extractor} node performs word-level overlap detection: for overlap lengths from \num{10} words down to~\num{1}, it compares the suffix of the previous chunk with the prefix of the current chunk.
An exact match returns the overlap length immediately; a \texttt{SequenceMatcher} fallback with similarity threshold \num{0.8} handles minor \gls{asr} variations in the overlap region (\eg \quotes{the quick brown} vs.\ \quotes{the quicker brown}).
Only the novel words beyond the detected overlap are emitted.
Without this deduplication, the \SI{300}{\second} window would contain approximately \SI{20}{\percent} redundant text, wasting context budget and biasing summaries toward repeated phrases.

\subsection{Window Aggregation}
\label{sec:arch:window}

The \texttt{window\_aggregator} node concatenates novel transcript segments into a rolling \SI{300}{\second} context window.
A window is flushed when $t_{\text{chunk\_end}} \ge t_{\text{window\_start}} + 300$.
On pipeline shutdown, partial windows are emitted to avoid data loss at stream end.

\subsection{Summarization}
\label{sec:arch:summ}

The \texttt{summarizer\_vllm} node loads model weights from a local filesystem path at warmup using vLLM's in-process \texttt{LLM} class~\cite{vllm}\td no daemon, no middleware, no network dependency at runtime.
The inference engine selection rationale is detailed in \cref{sec:impl:engine}.

We use Qwen3.5-4B~\cite{qwen3,qwen3-5-4b-awq} quantized to int4 via \gls{awq}~\cite{awq} at \num{4096} context length.
\Gls{awq} quantization roughly quarters the weight footprint relative to bfloat16, reducing inference latency and preserving KV-cache headroom for \SI{300}{\second} windows within the \SI{24}{\giga\byte} development \gls{gpu}.

The summarization prompt is designed with explicit alignment to the five Likert evaluation criteria.
\quotes{2--3 sentences capturing the main topics and conclusions} targets \emph{conciseness} and \emph{coherence}.
\quotes{Only state facts explicitly present in the transcript.\ Do not infer} targets \emph{factual consistency}.
\quotes{Exactly 3 keywords [\ldots] Never use generic terms like \textquotesingle discussion\textquotesingle, \textquotesingle meeting\textquotesingle, or \textquotesingle topic\textquotesingle} targets keyword relevance~($K_i$).
Chain-of-thought generation is suppressed (\texttt{enable\_thinking\allowbreak=False}), directly reducing output token count and thus $t_{\text{proc}}$.
Sampling parameters are set to temperature~$0.15$, top-$p = 0.9$, repetition penalty~$1.05$, and \texttt{max\_tokens}~$= 384$.

Two failure modes are handled without emitting an empty summary.
When the transcript is shorter than \num{80} characters (end-of-stream fragments), the \gls{llm} is skipped.
When the call raises an exception, returns unparseable JSON, or produces an empty summary, the pipeline falls back to an \emph{extractive summary}: the first sentence(s) of the transcript, capped at \num{300} characters.
This verbatim excerpt guarantees high factual consistency ($B_i$ cannot be penalized for a direct quote) and costs approximately \SI{0}{\milli\second}, preserving~$L_i$.
A warmup generation call during startup triggers CUDA kernel compilation before the first real window, preventing a cold-start latency spike.

\subsection{Keyword Validation}
\label{sec:arch:keywords}

Regardless of \gls{llm} output quality, the \texttt{ensure\_three\_keywords} function (\cref{alg:keywords}) guarantees exactly three keywords per window.
The algorithm first removes empty entries and filters against a set of banned generic terms (\quotes{meeting}, \quotes{discussion}, \quotes{topic}, \etc).
Case-insensitive deduplication preserves first-seen casing.
If fewer than three keywords remain, candidates are extracted from the transcript ranked by token frequency, with ties broken toward capitalized tokens (likely proper nouns).
As a last resort, conservative fallback terms are appended.

This converts a potential \num{12}-point swing in $K_i$ (from $-6$ to $+6$) into a \num{6}-point swing (from $0$ to $+6$): even when the \gls{llm} produces no usable keywords, the floor is $K_i = 0$ rather than $K_i = -6$.

\begin{algorithm}[htb!]
    \caption{Keyword guarantee: \texttt{ensure\_three\_keywords}.}
    \label{alg:keywords}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require $\mathit{kw}$: keyword list from \gls{llm}, $T$: transcript text
        \Ensure exactly 3 keyword strings
        \State $\mathit{kw} \gets$ strip whitespace, remove empty entries
        \State $\mathit{kw} \gets \{w \in \mathit{kw} \mid w_{\downarrow} \notin \textsc{Banned}\}$
        \State $\mathit{kw} \gets \text{deduplicate}(\mathit{kw}, \text{case-insensitive})$
        \If{$|\mathit{kw}| < 3$}
        \State $\mathit{cand} \gets$ rank tokens in $T$ by freq$\!\downarrow$, capitalization$\!\downarrow$
        \State filter $\mathit{cand}$: remove stopwords $\cup$ \textsc{Banned}
        \State append $\mathit{cand}$ to $\mathit{kw}$ until $|\mathit{kw}| = 3$
        \EndIf
        \If{$|\mathit{kw}| < 3$}
        \State pad $\mathit{kw}$ with fallback terms to length 3
        \EndIf
        \State \Return $\mathit{kw}[{:}3]$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Result Transmission}
\label{sec:arch:sink}

The \texttt{result\_transmitter} sink node maps internal keys to the challenge contract (\texttt{window\_start}~$\to$~\texttt{from}, \texttt{window\_end}~$\to$~\texttt{to}, \texttt{latency}~$\to$~\texttt{proc\_time}) and writes each window as \texttt{window\_N.json}.
When \texttt{destination\_endpoint} is configured, results are also POSTed via HTTP.
A debug mode persists the full transcript and raw \gls{llm} output alongside each window for post-hoc analysis.

\section{Implementation and Deployment}
\label{sec:implementation}

Pipeline configuration is assembled from two JSON files: \texttt{config-base.json} defines all nodes except the summarizer, and a profile from \texttt{pipelines/summarizer/} provides model-specific parameters (model path, sampling configuration, \gls{vram} budget).
A merge tool combines these at launch time, and a single shell script serves as the entry point for both development and submission.

The submission Docker image is based on NVIDIA CUDA 12.4 runtime with Python~3.12 and \texttt{uv} for dependency management.
Model weights for both the \gls{asr} model and the summarizer are baked into the image at build time, ensuring zero network dependency at runtime.
A \texttt{docker-compose.yml} file orchestrates two services: Janus forwards \gls{rtp} audio to the pipeline container on \gls{udp} port~\num{8888}.
\Cref{tab:nodes} summarizes all pipeline nodes and their key parameters.

\subsection{Inference Engine}
\label{sec:impl:engine}

We select vLLM~\cite{vllm} over alternative serving frameworks for three reasons aligned with the scoring formula and submission constraints.
First, its in-process library \gls{api} eliminates a separate inference server daemon: the submission container runs a single process with nothing to health-check or fail independently of the pipeline.
Second, paged attention holds $t_{\text{proc}}$ low across varying transcript lengths, which is the direct lever on $L_i = 10\,e^{-0.5 \cdot t_{\text{proc}}}$.
Third, the explicit \texttt{gpu\_memory\_utilization} parameter caps \gls{vram} usage, enabling the int4-\gls{awq} summarizer to co-reside with the \gls{asr} model within the \SI{24}{\giga\byte} development card.

Two alternatives were considered.
SGLang~\cite{sglang} (RadixAttention prefix caching) offers roughly \SI{29}{\percent} higher throughput than vLLM as a near drop-in replacement and remains a future upgrade path if $L_i$ becomes the binding constraint (\cref{sec:discussion}).
TensorRT-LLM achieves the highest raw throughput but requires per-model ahead-of-time compilation, which conflicts with iterative model selection during development and imposes significant overhead on the single-\gls{gpu} submission environment.
vLLM's broader model coverage and zero compilation overhead best match the challenge's single-\gls{gpu}, rapid-iteration context.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

The current results cover three podcast episodes from the Rev16 dataset~\cite{rev16}; full evaluation on the challenge audio set is ongoing and will expand this coverage.
Each \SI{300}{\second} window pipeline run produces approximately \num{5}~scored windows per episode.
All model comparisons use at least two independent pipeline runs per configuration to account for \gls{llm}-judge non-determinism; scores reported throughout are means across runs, with standard deviations where multiple runs are available.
The offline judge harness uses a cross-family panel of three int4-\gls{awq} judges\td Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct, Phi-4, and Gemma-3-27B-it\td none from the Qwen family of the summarizer, avoiding self-evaluation bias.
Each judge is loaded in its own subprocess and run sequentially after the summarizer pipeline exits; process exit reclaims \gls{vram} before the next judge loads, since the \SI{24}{\giga\byte} dev \gls{gpu} cannot hold the panel co-resident.
Following the G-Eval paradigm~\cite{geval}, every judge scores each summary on the five Likert criteria defined by the challenge and evaluates keyword relevance as boolean flags per keyword.
We report the panel consensus (mean and standard deviation across judges): low dispersion corroborates a score, while high dispersion flags judge-specific bias.
All experiments run on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 (\SI{24}{\giga\byte}, CUDA 12.4); the submission target is the larger RTX Pro 4500 (\SI{32}{\giga\byte}).

\gls{wer} is measured independently using jiwer against ground-truth transcripts.
Because the ground-truth text lacks timestamps, we employ proportional character-based alignment with a sliding-window search ($\pm\SI{30}{\percent}$ offsets) to find the best-matching reference segment for each window.
\gls{wer} is an independent signal of \gls{asr} quality and is not part of the $B_i / K_i / L_i$ scoring.

\subsection{ASR Model Comparison}
\label{sec:eval:asr}

\Cref{tab:asr-comparison} compares two faster-whisper variants across \num{19}~windows.
Both models achieve identical average \gls{wer} (\num{0.69}), yet \texttt{small.en} produces a higher final score (\num{40.00} vs.\ \num{39.73}).

This suggests that the summarizer's ability to extract coherent keywords depends more on vocabulary predictability than on transcription accuracy.

\subsection{Outlier Analysis}
\label{sec:eval:outlier}

At \SI{300}{\second} granularity, topic transitions and informal speech are averaged across a full five-minute window, smoothing the per-window quality variance seen at shorter durations.
In \cref{tab:per-window}, $B_i$ ranges only from \num{24.0} to \num{25.0} and $K_i$ is uniformly \num{6.0}; score differences are entirely latency-driven.
The primary remaining outlier risk is therefore a long, dense-speech window that inflates $t_{\text{proc}}$ (window~3: \SI{1.05}{\second}, $L_i = 5.91$), not a content-quality failure.
At shorter window durations (\eg \SI{30}{\second}), topic transitions produce $B_i$ drops of \num{3}--\num{6}~points and $K_i$ swings as large as $-4$, confirming that \SI{300}{\second} aggregation materially reduces per-window variance.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:eval:threats}

Several limitations affect the reported numbers.
The offline judge panel (Mistral-Small-24B, Phi-4, Gemma-3-27B, all int4-\gls{awq}) is a local approximation; the official challenge judge is hidden, so our scores should be treated as directional even where the panel agrees.
\Gls{llm} judges are non-deterministic: identical inputs yield scores differing by \num{0.1}--\num{0.9}~$C_i$~points across runs, and cross-audio content variation contributes an additional \num{0.9}--\num{1.5}~points of standard deviation; reported means over multiple runs mitigate but do not eliminate this noise.
Evaluation covers three podcast episodes from the Rev16 dataset; the held-out challenge audio may differ in speaker count, topic complexity, and acoustic conditions, and rankings may shift as coverage grows.
\gls{wer} measurement relies on proportional character alignment against untimestamped ground truth, introducing alignment noise.
Development hardware (RTX 4090, \SI{24}{\giga\byte}) is more \gls{vram}-constrained than the submission target (RTX Pro 4500, \SI{32}{\giga\byte}); models sized to fit the dev card fit the submission card with headroom.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The scoring formula's gating structure ($L_i = 0$ when $B_i < 10$) aligns well with practical meeting intelligence needs: users value accurate summaries over fast ones.
However, keyword scoring is disproportionately punitive: a single irrelevant keyword costs $-2$ points, creating a \num{4}-point swing per keyword.
This incentivizes conservative keyword strategies at the expense of topical specificity\td the keyword guarantee algorithm (\cref{alg:keywords}) is a direct response to this pressure.

The current summarization prompt is static across all windows.
Content-adaptive prompting that detects topic transitions or speaker changes could improve~$B_i$ on windows containing fragmented or rapidly-shifting content.
Several avenues for future work remain: migrating the summarizer to SGLang~\cite{sglang}, a near drop-in replacement reported at roughly \SI{29}{\percent} higher throughput than vLLM, would lower $t_{\text{proc}}$ and lift $L_i$ at fixed $B_i$; mixture-of-experts models (activating \SI{3}{\B} parameters per token from \SI{30}{\B} total capacity) could offer quality closer to a large model at the decode speed of a small one; encoder-decoder summarization models could achieve sub-\SI{100}{\milli\second} inference if their summary quality clears the $B_i \ge 10$ gate; and structured-output decoding could eliminate JSON parsing failures entirely.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a seven-node Juturna pipeline for the STREAMIND Grand Challenge that processes live Opus/\gls{rtp} audio into structured summaries and keywords in real time.
Three contributions target specific scoring terms: streaming chunk deduplication preserves transcript coherence~($B_i$), a keyword guarantee algorithm floors the keyword penalty at zero~($K_i$), and quality-gated latency optimization achieves \SI{0.79}{\second} average processing time~($L_i \approx 6.8$) while maintaining summary quality at ceiling ($B_i = 24$--$25$, $K_i = 6$ on all evaluated windows).
On current preliminary data (\num{3}~Rev16 episodes), the final source score for Qwen3.5-4B-AWQ at \SI{300}{\second} windows averages $40.14 \pm 0.85$ across episodes and runs, including the Janus bonus.
