\begin{abstract}
    Streaming summarization of live audio requires systems that maintain coherence across overlapping speech segments while satisfying strict per-window latency constraints.
    We address the \gls{streamind} Grand Challenge with an eight-node Juturna pipeline in which live Opus/RTP audio is ingested via the Janus WebRTC gateway, transcribed incrementally by faster-whisper, and summarized by Qwen3.5-4B-AWQ under vLLM with a \SI{300}{\second} rolling context window.
    Three design contributions target the challenge composite scoring.
    Streaming chunk deduplication removes overlap-induced redundancy to protect summary quality; a keyword guarantee algorithm enforces the three-keyword format and back-fills relevant terms from the transcript; and prompt engineering with thinking-mode suppression holds average processing time to \SI{0.76}{\second}.
    Across Rev16 podcast episodes, the pipeline achieves a cross-episode mean source score of $40.14 \pm 0.85$ out of \num{45}, with consistent scores on technical meeting sessions indicating generalization beyond the podcast domain.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\glsresetall

Live-audio summarization of meetings, conference sessions, and podcasts requires incremental outputs under strict latency constraints while maintaining coherence across overlapping speech segments.
The \gls{streamind} Grand Challenge\footnote{\url{https://attend.ieee.org/mmsp-2026/grand-challenges/}} formalizes this setting, requiring a system to process a live RTP audio stream and emit a structured summary with three keywords every \SI{300}{\second} window, scored jointly on summary quality, keyword relevance, and processing latency.

The challenge scores the output of each window $i$ as $C_i = B_i + K_i + L_i$, where $B_i \in [5, 25]$ measures summary quality across five Likert criteria, $K_i \in [-6, +6]$ rewards relevant keywords and penalizes irrelevant ones, and the latency bonus $L_i = 10 \cdot e^{-0.5 \cdot \text{proc}_i}$ rewards faster processing.
When $B_i < \num{10}$, this bonus is withheld regardless of processing speed.
\cref{fig:scoring} illustrates the $L_i$ score under changing processing times.
Moreover, per challenge description, using the Janus WebRTC gateway~\cite{janus} as the audio source adds a flat $+4$ bonus to the overall final score.
This scoring structure dictates a clear design priority, namely to drive $B_i$ above the gate first, minimize $\text{proc}_i$ at fixed quality, and treat any $K_i$ regression as unacceptable since one irrelevant keyword erases multiple points of latency gain.

\begin{figure}[htb!]
    \centering
    \input{figures/scoring-curve}
    \caption{Latency bonus $L_i = 10 \, e^{-0.5 \cdot \text{proc}_i}$, gated by $B_i \ge 10$. Below the gate, latency contributes zero; above it, returns diminish as processing time increases.}
    \label{fig:scoring}
\end{figure}

To address the \gls{streamind} Grand Challenge, we build an open-source real-time multimedia pipeline and present three contributions, each targeting a specific scoring term:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \item A streaming chunk deduplication strategy that preserves temporal coherence across overlapping \SI{5}{\second} \gls{asr} chunks while keeping the aggregated \SI{300}{\second} window non-redundant~($B_i$).
    \item A keyword guarantee algorithm that strips banned generic terms and back-fills missing slots from transcript-derived terms, enforcing the challenge three-keyword requirement so that format failures never forfeit the keyword score~$K_i$.
    \item Quality-gated latency optimization through prompt engineering and thinking-mode suppression, maintaining $B_i$ above \num{23} of \num{25} while achieving \SI{0.76}{\second} average processing time ($L_i \approx 6.8$).
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
\Cref{sec:related} surveys \gls{asr} and \gls{llm} inference options and situates the work among prior streaming meeting summarization pipelines.
\Cref{sec:architecture} details the eight pipeline nodes and their role.
\Cref{sec:implementation} covers inference-engine selection and submission configuration.
\Cref{sec:evaluation} reports \gls{asr} model comparison, per-window scoring, component ablations, and the model-size and cross-family sweep.
\Cref{sec:conclusion} discusses scoring trade-offs, summarizes contributions, and outlines future directions.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Building a live audio summarization system for the \gls{streamind} Grand Challenge draws on three components, namely the Juturna~\cite{juturna} framework, the optional Janus~\cite{janus} gateway, and \gls{llm} inference for summarization and keyword extraction.

Juturna~\cite{juturna} is an open-source Python library for composing real-time multimedia pipelines from typed, message-passing nodes arranged as a directed acyclic graph in JSON.
Each node is driven through a four-stage lifecycle (\texttt{configure} $\to$ \texttt{warmup} $\to$ \texttt{start} $\to$ \texttt{stop}), and internally separates reception, buffering, and processing into independent threads, which enables stages to absorb backpressure without stalling upstream data flow.
Nodes take one of three roles, \emph{source} (data ingestion), \emph{processing} (transform), or \emph{sink} (delivery), and exchange strongly-typed payloads along directed edges.
Janus~\cite{janus} is an open-source, general-purpose WebRTC server that handles session negotiation and forwards decoded media as plain RTP to downstream consumers.
The built-in \texttt{audio\_rtp} source in Juturna integrates with the Janus RTP endpoint, receiving mono Opus/\SI{48}{\kilo\hertz} audio~\cite{rtp,opus}.

To transcribe the incoming audio, the pipeline builds on the Whisper model family~\cite{whisper}, which achieves broad multilingual coverage through weak supervision on large-scale web audio.
CTranslate2-based~\cite{ctranslate} reimplementations such as \texttt{faster-whisper}~\cite{faster-whisper} reduce inference latency relative to the reference implementation while preserving accuracy at \texttt{float16} precision on the GPU.

The resulting transcripts are summarized by instruction-tuned \glspl{llm}, which can produce structured summaries and keyword lists in a single inference call~\cite{qwen3}.
Several inference backends serve such models with different trade-offs.
vLLM~\cite{vllm} pairs paged attention and continuous batching with an in-process \texttt{LLM} class that needs no separate inference daemon and installs from mature prebuilt wheels.
SGLang~\cite{sglang} reports around \SI{29}{\percent} higher throughput through RadixAttention prefix caching, but that advantage targets high-concurrency, shared-prefix serving rather than a single live stream, and its serving stack carries heavier build dependencies.
TensorRT-LLM~\cite{tensor-rt-llm} maximizes raw throughput through per-model ahead-of-time compilation, which impedes rapid model iteration, whereas llama.cpp~\cite{llamacpp} targets portable CPU inference via GGUF quantization rather than the GPU.
For a single-stream, rapidly-iterated, offline-packaged challenge entry, the vLLM zero-compilation install and self-contained execution outweigh the SGLang throughput edge.
On the latency curve (\cref{fig:scoring}), even a full \SI{29}{\percent} latency translation lifts $L_i$ by under one point at sub-second processing.

Offline evaluation of \gls{llm}-generated summaries follows the \gls{llm}-as-judge paradigm~\cite{geval}, scoring outputs against source transcripts on multiple quality criteria, with judges drawn from distinct model families to reduce single-model bias.

The components above situate within a body of prior work on real-time \gls{asr} and meeting summarization that informs three aspects of our design.
Streaming transcription approaches include Whisper adapted via a local agreement policy~\cite{machacek2023} and dialect-specific pipelines that couple \gls{asr} with direct summarization~\cite{saranya2025}.
Our pipeline instead uses fixed overlapping chunks with greedy suffix--prefix deduplication rather than adaptive windowing.
On pipeline structure, cascade \gls{asr}+\gls{llm} systems have been validated sentence-wise at Interspeech~\cite{matsuura2024} and integrated with extractive NLP as unified audio-to-summary applications~\cite{whispersum2024}.
Liu and Chen~\cite{liu2021sliding} apply a dynamic sliding window to long meeting transcripts.
The challenge specification mandates a fixed \SI{300}{\second} window granularity, and we extend this design with in-process \gls{llm} inference in place of extractive NLP.
Asthana \etal~\cite{asthana2024} validate structured \gls{llm} output for post-meeting recap, informing our prompt design.
Schneider \etal~\cite{schneider2025} formalize quality-latency trade-offs for online summarization.
Our system embeds these trade-offs as the $B_i$-gated $L_i$ scoring term per challenge rather than an abstract policy objective.
Collectively, these designs establish the building blocks our pipeline assembles into a scoring-formula-aware, end-to-end live challenge system.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\Cref{fig:pipeline} shows the eight-node pipeline; each node targets one or more terms of $C_i = B_i + K_i + L_i$.
Janus provides WebRTC audio ingestion; the hallucination filter and novel extractor deliver a clean, non-redundant transcript that protects $B_i$; keyword validation enforces the three-keyword format to avoid penalty in $K_i$; and the summarizer balances output quality against processing time at the $B_i$/$L_i$ interface.

    \centering
    \input{figures/pipeline}
    \caption{End-to-end \gls{streamind} pipeline. Janus forwards Opus/RTP media into the Juturna \texttt{audio\_rtp} source node; \SI{5}{\second} chunks with a \SI{4}{\second} advance are transcribed by \gls{asr}; hallucination filtering and novel extraction precede a \SI{300}{\second} aggregation buffer; the summarizer emits output per window; the result sink stores locally and posts to the challenge endpoint.}

\end{figure*}

\subsection{Audio Reception}
\label{sec:arch:audio}

Janus~\cite{janus} handles the challenge WebRTC session and forwards mono Opus/\SI{48}{\kilo\hertz} RTP packets to the Juturna \texttt{audio\_rtp} source node on UDP port~\num{8888}, matching the challenge stream specification.

\subsection{Incremental ASR}
\label{sec:arch:asr}

%TOOD: wait then our latency is 5 seconds + LLM latency????
The \texttt{audio\_chunker} node buffers the continuous audio stream into \SI{5}{\second} chunks with \SI{1}{\second} overlap, yielding a \SI{4}{\second} advance per step.
Each chunk is transcribed by the \texttt{transcriber\_whisper} node running the \gls{asr} model faster-whisper-large-v3-turbo~\cite{faster-whisper-large-v3-turbo-ct2} at \texttt{float16} precision on the GPU, occupying around \SI{2}{\giga\byte} of VRAM.
The distilled model and the summarizer model together fit within the \SI{24}{\giga\byte} development card with headroom for the \gls{kv} cache.
Previous-text conditioning is disabled to prevent hallucination propagation across chunks, and \gls{vad} filtering via Silero~\cite{silero-vad} skips silence segments.

\subsection{Hallucination Filter}
\label{sec:arch:halluc}

The \texttt{hallucination\_filter} node applies two post-\gls{asr} guards on the transcription.
First, inline noise tags (\eg \texttt{[Music]}, \texttt{(applause)}) are stripped via regex patterns.
Second, the text is checked against \num{22} known Whisper hallucination patterns (\eg \quotes{please subscribe}, \quotes{like and subscribe}) and discarded if it matches one.
Without this filter, hallucinated sentences reach the summarizer and are reproduced as facts in the generated summary, inflating context token usage, increasing processing latency, and penalizing the factual consistency component of~$B_i$.

\subsection{Novel Chunk Extraction}
\label{sec:arch:novel}

The \SI{1}{\second} overlap between consecutive chunks means that this interval of speech appears in both the current and previous transcription.
The \texttt{novel\_extractor} node performs word-level overlap detection by greedily testing suffix–prefix matches from length \num{10} words down to~\num{1} and accepting the first exact match found.
An exact match returns the overlap length; a \texttt{SequenceMatcher} fallback with similarity threshold \num{0.8} handles minor \gls{asr} variations in the overlap region (\eg \quotes{the quick brown} vs.\ \quotes{the quicker brown}).
Only the novel words beyond the detected overlap are emitted.
Without this deduplication, the \SI{300}{\second} window would contain around \SI{20}{\percent} redundant text, wasting context budget and biasing summaries toward repeated phrases.

\subsection{Window Aggregation}
\label{sec:arch:window}

The \texttt{window\_aggregator} node concatenates incoming novel segments into a rolling \SI{300}{\second} context buffer, matching the challenge scoring granularity.
A window is flushed and forwarded to the summarizer when $t_{\text{chunk\_end}} \ge t_{\text{window\_start}} + 300$.
On pipeline shutdown, any partial window accumulated since the last flush is emitted as well, ensuring the final audio segment contributes a scored output rather than being dropped.

\subsection{Summarization}
\label{sec:arch:summ}

At warmup, the \texttt{summarizer\_vllm} node loads model weights from a local filesystem path through the vLLM in-process \texttt{LLM} class~\cite{vllm}, eliminating any inference daemon or runtime network dependency.
Paged attention keeps $\text{proc}_i$ low across variable transcript lengths, a direct lever on $L_i$, and the explicit \texttt{gpu\_memory\_utilization} budget lets the summarizer co-reside with the \gls{asr} model on the development GPU.
A dummy generation call at startup triggers CUDA kernel compilation before the first live window, removing the cold-start spike that would otherwise inflate $\text{proc}_i$.

We utilize Qwen3.5-4B~\cite{qwen3,qwen3-5-4b-awq} quantized to \texttt{int4} via \gls{awq}~\cite{awq} at \num{4096} context length.
\Gls{awq} quantization roughly quarters the weight footprint relative to \texttt{bfloat16}, reducing inference latency and preserving \gls{kv} cache headroom for \SI{300}{\second} windows.

The summarization prompt targets the five Likert evaluation criteria.
\emph{2--3 sentences capturing the main topics and conclusions} targets conciseness and coherence.
\emph{Only state facts explicitly present in the transcript.\ Do not infer} targets factual consistency.
\emph{Exactly 3 keywords [\ldots] Never use generic terms like \textquotesingle discussion\textquotesingle, \textquotesingle meeting\textquotesingle, or \textquotesingle topic\textquotesingle} targets keyword relevance~($K_i$).
Chain-of-thought generation is suppressed via \texttt{enable\_thinking\allowbreak=False}, reducing output token count and thus $\text{proc}_i$.
Sampling parameters are set conservatively, with temperature~$0.15$ and top-$p = 0.9$ promoting deterministic, on-topic outputs, repetition penalty~$1.05$ discouraging verbatim transcript echoing, and \texttt{max\_tokens}~$= 384$ capping the generation length.

Two failure modes are handled to ensure the pipeline never emits an empty summary.
When the transcript is shorter than \num{80} characters (\eg silence), the \gls{llm} is skipped.
When the call raises an exception, returns unparseable JSON, or produces an empty summary, the pipeline falls back to an \emph{extractive summary} consisting of the first sentence(s) of the transcript, capped at \num{300} characters.
This verbatim excerpt guarantees high factual consistency ($B_i$ cannot be penalized for a direct quote) and incurs negligible processing time, preserving~$L_i$.

\subsection{Keyword Validation}
\label{sec:arch:keywords}

The \texttt{ensure\_three\_keywords} function guarantees three keywords per window, regardless of \gls{llm} output quality.
The algorithm first removes empty entries and filters out banned generic terms (\quotes{meeting}, \quotes{discussion}, \quotes{topic}, \etc).
Case-insensitive deduplication preserves first-seen casing.
If fewer than three keywords remain, candidates are extracted from the transcript ranked by token frequency, with ties broken toward capitalized tokens (\eg proper nouns).
As a last resort, conservative fallback terms are appended.

By always emitting three transcript-grounded keywords, the guarantee enforces format compliance and removes the empty-output and wrong-count failure modes.
It does not floor the keyword score, however, which the challenge defines as $K_i = 2\,(N_\text{rel} - N_\text{irrel})$ over the three keywords; a summary whose fallback terms are all judged irrelevant can still reach $K_i = -6$.
Since transcript-derived terms inherit the source topic, they score as relevant and keep $K_i$ high in practice, an effect analyzed across model families in \cref{sec:evaluation}.

\subsection{Result Transmission}
\label{sec:arch:sink}

% TODO: complete on the code. Mongo?

The \texttt{result\_transmitter} sink node maps internal keys to the challenge contract (\texttt{from}, \texttt{to}, \texttt{summary}, \texttt{keywords}, \texttt{proc\_time}) and saves each window as \texttt{window\_i.json}.
When \texttt{destination\_endpoint} is configured, results are also posted via HTTP.
A debug mode persists the full transcript and raw \gls{llm} output alongside each window for post-hoc analysis during development.

\section{Implementation and Deployment}
\label{sec:implementation}

Pipeline configuration is split across two JSON files to decouple node topology from model-specific parameters.
\texttt{config-base.json} declares all nodes except the summarizer; a profile from \texttt{pipelines/summarizer/} supplies the model path, sampling configuration, and VRAM budget.
A merge tool assembles these at launch, and a single shell script serves as the entry point across development and submission.

The submission Docker image builds on the NVIDIA CUDA~\num{12.4} runtime with Python~\num{3.12}, embedding the weights for both the \gls{asr} model and the summarizer at build time so the image carries no runtime network dependency.
Since the summarizer runs vLLM in-process (\cref{sec:related,sec:arch:summ}) rather than as a separate service, the pipeline and its summarizer ship as a single self-contained container with no inference daemon to orchestrate.
A \texttt{docker-compose.yml} file then adds Janus alongside this container as the only companion service.
We release the full implementation as open source.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/streaming-university/streamind}}

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate the pipeline against the composite challenge scoring, isolating the contribution of each design decision rather than reporting a single aggregate.
On the Rev16 podcast evaluation, the submission configuration (Qwen3.5-4B-AWQ summarizer with \texttt{large-v3-turbo} \gls{asr}) attains a final source score of $40.14 \pm 0.85$ out of \num{45}, holding $K_i$ at its ceiling on every window while keeping average processing time under \SI{1}{\second}.
In this section, we detail the measurement methodology, compare \gls{asr} models, report per-window scoring, ablate individual components, and sweep summarizer families, before stating threats to validity.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

Experiments cover the podcast episodes from the Rev16 dataset~\cite{rev16}.
Each pipeline run partitions an episode into consecutive \SI{300}{\second} windows, each scored independently.
To absorb \gls{llm}-judge non-determinism, every model comparison repeats the pipeline at least three times per configuration.
The numbers reported throughout are means across these runs, accompanied by standard deviations.
The offline judge harness uses a cross-family panel of three int4-\gls{awq} judges (Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct, Phi-4, and Gemma-3-27B-IT) chosen from outside the Qwen family to avoid self-evaluation bias.
Judges run sequentially in isolated subprocesses; each process exits to reclaim VRAM before the next loads, since the \SI{24}{\giga\byte} development GPU cannot host the full panel simultaneously.
Following the G-Eval paradigm~\cite{geval}, each judge scores every summary against the five Likert criteria defined by the challenge and evaluates keyword relevance as a per-keyword boolean flag.
Panel consensus is reported as the mean and standard deviation across the three judges.
Low dispersion lends confidence to a score, while high dispersion flags judge-specific bias and is treated with caution.
All experiments ran on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 with \SI{24}{\giga\byte} of VRAM under CUDA 12.4, while the submission target is the larger RTX Pro 4500 with \SI{32}{\giga\byte}.

\Gls{wer}, which is not part of the $B_i / K_i / L_i$ scoring formula, serves as an independent diagnostic and is measured via jiwer against ground-truth transcripts.
Since the ground-truth text lacks timestamps, proportional character-based alignment with a sliding-window search ($\pm\SI{30}{\percent}$ offsets) locates the best-matching reference segment for each window.

\label{sec:eval:asr}

\subsection{Outlier Analysis}
\label{sec:eval:outlier}

Since $B_i$ and $K_i$ stay flat across the windows of \cref{tab:per-window}, the residual $C_i$ variation is latency-driven rather than a content-quality failure; the sole outlier risk is a dense-speech window that inflates $\text{proc}_i$.
In exploratory shorter-window runs, topic transitions produced $B_i$ drops of \num{3}--\num{6}~points and $K_i$ swings as large as $-4$, variance that the \SI{300}{\second} aggregation averages out.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:eval:ablation}

The hallucination filter, novel extractor, and keyword guarantee (\cref{sec:arch:halluc,sec:arch:novel,sec:arch:keywords}) each defend a specific scoring term; we assess their effect qualitatively here.
The keyword guarantee is the most consequential.
For Qwen3.5-4B-AWQ, it holds $K_i$ at the maximum of \num{6.0} on every evaluated window, and the enforced format compliance removes the empty-output failure mode outright.
It sets no hard floor, however.
Under the weaker Llama-3.1-8B summarizer, two windows of episode~\num{11} still scored $K_i = -6$ under the Gemma judge, where transcript-derived fallbacks inherited the low-relevance signal of poorly summarized content.
Upstream, the hallucination filter and novel extractor protect $B_i$ by suppressing the \num{22} known artifact phrases and the $\approx\SI{20}{\percent}$ overlap redundancy before either can distort the summary.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:eval:threats}

Several limitations affect the reported numbers.
Our offline judge panel of Mistral-Small-24B, Phi-4, and Gemma-3-27B (all int4-\gls{awq}) is a proxy for the hidden challenge judge, so even where the three agree the reported scores are directional rather than absolute.
Repeated runs on identical inputs produce $C_i$ values that differ by \num{0.1}--\num{0.9} points, and content variation across audio episodes contributes a further \num{0.9}--\num{1.5} points of score variance.
Averaging across runs reduces judge noise but not content-driven variance.
Evaluation covers Rev16 podcast episodes and \gls{ietf}~125 technical sessions (\cref{sec:eval:scoring}); the held-out challenge audio may differ in speaker count, topic complexity, and acoustic conditions; therefore, its ranking is not guaranteed to match.
\gls{wer} measurement relies on proportional character alignment against untimestamped ground truth, which introduces alignment noise on top of the underlying transcription errors.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The scoring formula couples quality and speed asymmetrically.
The latency gate withholds any $L_i$ reward until $B_i \ge 10$, and each irrelevant keyword costs $-2$ points, on the order of a \SI{0.5}{\second} latency gain.
Our pipeline answers this ordering, securing $B_i$ above the gate and protecting $K_i$ before reducing $\text{proc}_i$.

The resulting eight-node Juturna pipeline summarizes live Opus/RTP audio in real time, averaging $40.14 \pm 0.85$ out of \num{45} across Rev16 episodes.
Three mechanisms drive this result: ($i$) streaming chunk deduplication protects $B_i$, ($ii$) the keyword guarantee secures the format underlying $K_i$, and ($iii$) thinking-mode suppression with int4-\gls{awq} quantization holds $\text{proc}_i$ low for $L_i$.
Our pipeline holds $B_i$ at $24$--$25$ and $K_i$ at its ceiling while keeping processing time near \SI{0.76}{\second}, leaving latency as its main remaining lever.

We also explored supervised fine-tuning of the Qwen3.5-4B summarizer on the Rev16 episodes, adapting the base model with QLoRA~\cite{qlora} on a teacher-distilled dataset of judge-filtered summary and keyword targets.
These experiments predate the final prompt and were run against an earlier prompt under which the base model scored $B_i \approx \num{19}$, so their absolute $B_i$ is not directly comparable to the finalized-prompt results.
Across three variants spanning teacher capacity and label coverage, the strongest, distilled entirely from a high-capacity teacher, lifted $B_i$ to $\approx\num{21}$ but lengthened the outputs, pushing $\text{proc}_i$ from $\approx\SI{0.7}{\second}$ to $\approx\SI{1.05}{\second}$ and regressing $L_i$.
Refining the prompt alone (\cref{sec:arch:summ}) instead raised the base model to $B_i \approx \num{24}$ at sub-second latency (\cref{tab:per-window}), placing it ahead of every fine-tuned variant on the final source score.
The submission therefore ships the base summarizer with the finalized prompt rather than tuned weights, consistent with the asymmetric scoring this work targets, in which a quality gain that costs processing time is penalized.

If concurrency grows or the latency budget tightens, an SGLang backend could reclaim part of that margin without affecting $B_i$.
A second direction hardens robustness, replacing JSON parsing with structured-output decoding and adapting the prompt to topic transitions to curb the $B_i$ variance seen on dense windows.
